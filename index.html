Block 1 · Problem

73 days, not 45

Legal limit 45 days. Nearly 2 in 3 invoices paid late.

Sources: Recordent, Indian SME Receivables Report 2026 (73 days); MSMED Act 2006, s.15 (45 days); Atradius, Payment Practices Barometer India 2025 (2 in 3).

[~35 s] Block one: the problem. The law allows a buyer 45 days to pay a small supplier. In practice the average is 73. And nearly two in three B2B credit invoices arrive late (Atradius puts it at 63 percent). It is worst in textiles, where write-offs reach up to 10 percent, and in chemical clusters, where more than 70 percent of invoices are paid late. [→ one press: hand-off animation, then the next slide follows by itself.]

The insight

Not a payment problem.

A relationship problem.

Illustrative scenario. Legal limit: MSMED Act 2006, s.15. 73 days: Recordent, Indian SME Receivables Report 2026.

[~60 s for the whole slide · setup] Here is a typical distributor: one supplier above, and ten retailers who buy on credit. This is an illustrative picture, not data. [Click 1] Goods go out on credit, udhaar, and every retailer's ledger starts at day zero. [Click 2] Cash is supposed to come back. Three pay on time, four pay part of it, and three simply don't. [Click 3] The law gives a buyer 45 days. In practice the average is 73. Meanwhile the distributor's cash drains, and the supplier wants to be paid. [Click 4] So the owner faces a dilemma. Push hard, and the relationship snaps: the customer is lost. Stay polite, and the money stays stuck. [Click 5] That is why this is not a payment problem. It is a relationship problem. So what if someone else made that call, politely? That is our solution. [→ one press: hand-off animation, then the next slide follows by itself.]

[~40 s] Block four: the solution. The owner shares the ledger, from Tally, Vyapar, or even a photograph of a handwritten khata. Our AI assistant calls each buyer politely, in the buyer's own language (Telugu, Hindi or Tamil), agrees a realistic part-payment, and sends a UPI link during the call. The owner receives a daily report: who paid, who promised, who needs attention. Nothing escalates without the owner's approval. [Click] So a promise becomes a payment within minutes. Courtesy is not a weakness here. It is the product. [→ one press: hand-off animation, then the next slide follows by itself.]

Blocks 6 + 7 · Revenue · Costs

₹4,689 a month

per client: ₹999 + 3% of recovered. 67% margin.

Per client per month, assuming ₹2,00,000 recovered: pays ₹6,999, costs ₹2,310. Planning assumptions.

[~40 s] Blocks six and seven: how we earn, and what we spend. We charge ₹999 a month plus three percent of what we recover, so we earn when our client gets paid. A typical client pays about ₹7,000 a month. Serving that client costs about ₹2,300, mostly AI call minutes and messages. We keep ₹4,689: a contribution margin of 67 percent. We need about ₹4.9 lakh to start. Running costs rise in steps, from under one lakh a month in the pilot to about ₹3.7 lakh when we expand to Gujarat. We rent voice, telephony and payments, and build only what differentiates us. [→ one press: hand-off animation, then the next slide follows by itself.]

Block 8 · Key Metrics

Break-even at 56 clients

Schematic from planning assumptions: ₹2 lakh recovered per client per month; fixed costs of about ₹2.6 lakh a month.

[~40 s] Block eight: the numbers that tell us whether the model works. The most important is money recovered per client; our plan assumes two lakh rupees a month. We also track promises kept (pilot pass mark: 40 percent of contacted buyers) and days to get paid, against today's 73. The green line is what clients contribute; the red line is our fixed cost. Where they cross, we break even: about 56 clients, in month sixteen. [Click] If recovery were half our assumption, the slope flattens and we would need 154 clients. That is why the pilot measures recovery first. [→ one press: hand-off animation, then the next slide follows by itself.]

Block 5 · Channels

₹4,200 Cr market

5 lakh distributors, reached through CAs, trade bodies and Tally.

TAM = 5 lakh distributors (FAIDA) × ₹84,000 a year. SAM ₹420 Cr (10%), SOM ₹4.2 Cr (1% of SAM, Year 4): planning assumptions.

[~35 s] Block five: channels, and the market they open. India has about five lakh distributors. At about ₹84,000 a year, that is a ₹4,200 crore market. We start where the pain is worst: chemicals, agri-food and textiles. That serviceable segment is about ₹420 crore, and we aim for 500 clients, about ₹4.2 crore a year, by year four. We reach them through people they already trust: chartered accountants with 50-plus MSME clients, trade associations, the Tally and Vyapar ecosystems, and door-to-door visits in Begum Bazaar. Winning a client costs about ₹14,000, earned back in three months. [→ one press: hand-off animation, then the next slide follows by itself.]

Questions

[The ball from the last slide traces the question mark.] [When questions are done, click once: the slide fills with question marks, fades to dark, and Thank you follows by itself.] I would welcome your questions. Prepared answers: • Why would a shopkeeper answer an AI call? The call comes from the supplier's registered business name, in the buyer's language. And we test this before automating: the first pilot calls are made by us, by hand. • What stops Khatabook or Vyapar from copying you? For them, reminders are a free feature. For us, collection is the whole business: pay-on-results pricing, people in the markets, and the payment data we build up over time. • Is it legal to call a supplier's buyers? Yes. A supplier may follow up its own dues. We use registered caller IDs, call only between 8 am and 7 pm, the AI says it is an AI, and data agreements follow the DPDP Act. • Why assume ₹2 lakh recovered per client per month? Our early adopters hold ₹10 lakh or more overdue, so that is about a fifth of the backlog each month. The pilot replaces this assumption with real data. • What if the seed grant does not come through? Stage 0 costs under ₹1 lakh a month and runs on the founders' ₹3 lakh. The angel round would then be sized up to bridge the gap. • Why are you the right person to build this? My background is AI and data science, and I have built CRM-integrated AI systems and data pipelines in industry. I am based in Hyderabad, next to our first market.
